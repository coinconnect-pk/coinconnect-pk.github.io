Part A of Form I is where a [sandbox](https://www.coinconnect.site/blog/coinconnect-insights-1/pvara-sandbox-reduced-capital-pakistan-19) application succeeds or fails on paper before [PVARA](https://www.coinconnect.site/pvara-guide) ever assesses whether the applicant is ready to test. It is the section that describes what the innovation is, what technology carries it, how it is secured, what law governs it, and what could go wrong. Get Part A vague, and the rest of the application reads as guesswork built on an undefined foundation.

The Sandbox Guidelines 2026 lay out Form I in six parts, A through F. This article covers Part A in full — five sub-sections, each with its own required format and attachment expectation, drawn directly from the Guidelines' own table.

## What is Form I Part A for?

Part A asks the applicant to establish, in writing, what the innovation actually is, on what technology it runs, how it is secured, what regulatory regime it sits inside, and what could go wrong — before PVARA looks at whether the team is ready to test it. The Guidelines head this section:

> "A. Innovation / Virtual Asset Service Provider Proposition"

Every one of its five sub-sections requires a supporting attachment except none — all five are marked "Y" for attachments required in the Guidelines' table, which signals that PVARA expects documentary depth here, not summary paragraphs alone.

## What must the service and innovation summary cover?

The first sub-section is a concise description of the innovation, targeted at 500 to 1,000 words, with an attachment required. The Guidelines list it as:

> "1. Service to be offered including Summary of the Innovation — Concise description of your innovation. ~500-1000 words. Y"

At this length, the summary has to do real work: what the service is, who it serves, and what makes it new. The Key Evaluation Criteria elsewhere in the Guidelines assess "Novelty of the model," "Harnessing technology," "Differentiation" from existing offerings, and "Inclusion" — described as helping "transition a largely informal and high-risk market into a formalized, regulated safe ecosystem." A summary that does not speak to at least the first three of those four themes is leaving marks on the table.

## What does the blockchain and technology stack section require?

This sub-section asks for the distributed ledger technology (DLT) used, whether public or permissioned, the smart contract platform, wallet architecture, throughput and scalability — presented in bullet points or a table, with an attachment required. The Guidelines specify:

> "2. Blockchain / Technology Stack — DLT used (public/permissioned), smart contract platform, wallet architecture, throughput, scalability. Bullet points / table. Y"

The Act's own definition of the underlying technology is worth anchoring this section to. Section 3 of the [Virtual Assets Act](https://blog.coinconnect.site/pakistans-virtual-assets-act-2026-the-complete-guide/), 2026 defines blockchain or DLT as "a technology that enables a distributed ledger, an information repository that records transactions or data across multiple nodes in a synchronized manner using cryptography to ensure integrity, tamper-resistance, immutability, and consensus among participants, as may be further defined by Regulations." A technology stack description that maps cleanly onto that definition — naming the consensus mechanism, the synchronisation model, the cryptographic integrity controls — gives evaluators less room to ask clarifying questions later.

## What must the cybersecurity strategy include?

Part A requires a threat model, security controls, incident response, disaster recovery, and privacy and data protection measures, presented as a table plus narrative, with an attachment required:

> "3. Cybersecurity Strategy — Threat model, security controls (encryption, key management, secure coding, audits), incident response, disaster recovery, privacy/data protection. Table + narrative. Y"

This sub-section is not decorative. The Annexure-A self-assessment checklist used alongside Form I treats weak cybersecurity as a direct disqualifier, listing "Weak cybersecurity indicators, no audits, or reliance on unverified smart contracts" and "Lack of safeguards to ensure [custody](https://www.coinconnect.site/blog/coinconnect-insights-1/pvara-custody-license-safeguarding-customer-assets-25) of consumer's funds in case of cyber intrusions" as negative indicators under its Technology & Security question. Independent third-party security testing is named as a positive indicator in the same checklist. An applicant with a completed smart contract audit or third-party penetration test should attach the report itself here, not merely describe that testing occurred.

## What goes in the regulatory and legal environment sub-section?

This sub-section asks for the applicable laws and regulations, ML/TF/PF obligations (money laundering, terrorist financing and proliferation financing), consumer protection rules, and intellectual property and data protection law — as bullet points, with an attachment required:

> "4. Regulatory / Legal Environment — Applicable laws/regulations, ML/TF/PF obligations, consumer protection, IP/data protection laws. Bullet points. Y"

This is the section where the application demonstrates it understands the full regulatory perimeter around the product, not only the sandbox itself. Under section 46 of the Act, [virtual asset](https://www.coinconnect.site/blog/coinconnect-insights-1/pvara-license-pakistan-complete-guide-22) service providers licensed under the Act are deemed financial institutions for the purposes of the [Anti-Money Laundering](https://www.coinconnect.site/blog/3/fit-and-proper-aml-pvara-mistakes-54) Act, 2010, with obligations to report suspicious transactions, maintain due-diligence records, and run internal AML, CFT and CPF controls. An applicant whose Part A submission does not reference this framework, or the FMU's [goAML registration](https://www.coinconnect.site/blog/coinconnect-insights-1/fmu-goaml-vasp-pakistan-17) requirement that flows from it, is leaving a gap an evaluator will notice.

## How should the risk management table be structured?

Part A's final sub-section requires financial, operational, cybersecurity, legal and regulatory, market, and reputational risks, each paired with its mitigant, in table format, with an attachment required:

> "5. Risk Management — Financial, operational, cybersecurity, legal/regulatory, market and reputational risks with mitigants. Table format. Y"

The Guidelines name six risk categories explicitly, and the table format is specified, not optional. A risk register that lists a risk without a paired mitigant does not meet the stated format. Practical structure for the table:

- One row per identified risk, tagged to one of the six named categories.
- A plain description of the risk in the applicant's own operating context, not a generic industry risk statement.
- A specific, named mitigant — a control, a policy, a technical safeguard, or a monitoring process — not a general statement of intent to manage the risk.
- Where relevant, a cross-reference to the cybersecurity strategy in sub-section 3, since several risks in this table will share mitigants with that section.

## How does Part A connect to the rest of Form I?

Part A establishes what the innovation is and what governs it; the remaining parts of Form I build on that foundation rather than repeating it. Part B tests readiness to actually run the test described in Part A. Part C asks how the test ends. Parts D and E cover the applicant's own background and particulars.

| Part | Relationship to Part A |
|---|---|
| B — Readiness for testing | Assumes the technology stack and cybersecurity strategy from Part A are accurate; asks for evidence they are operational |
| C — Exit strategy and scaling | Builds the wind-down and scale-up plan around the specific innovation described in Part A |
| D — Applicant background | Establishes whether the team has the expertise the technology described in Part A actually requires |
| E — Applicant particulars | Confirms the [Schedule I](https://www.coinconnect.site/blog/coinconnect-insights-1/pvara-license-categories-explained-23) category the innovation falls under, based on what Part A described |

A Part A submission that describes one product but a Part B submission that evidences readiness for a different one is the kind of internal inconsistency PVARA's evaluators are positioned to catch, given that the Guidelines direct assessment to be based on "submission at the time of the application or any other information sought subsequently."

## What should an applicant do before submitting Part A?

Draft Part A last, even though it appears first in the form. Every other part of Form I depends on Part A's description being precise, so finalising it only after the technology, risk and compliance work behind it is settled avoids having to revise a submitted description mid-application — and the Guidelines only permit "up to two resubmissions" for an incomplete application before it is returned outright.

Before submission, confirm each of the five sub-sections has its required attachment, that the risk table names a specific mitigant for every listed risk, and that the regulatory and legal environment section speaks directly to Pakistan's framework rather than a generic international summary.
