Every other criminal penalty in the [Virtual Assets Act](https://blog.coinconnect.site/pakistans-virtual-assets-act-2026-the-complete-guide/) 2026 has a fixed Rupee ceiling — fifty million here, twenty-five million there. Section 54(3)(b) does not. It is the single provision in the Act where a company's exposure is calculated from its own financial results, and it can, in principle, run to any figure at all.

This is the penalty for a legal person that wilfully contravenes section 52 — the prohibition on market manipulation, market abuse and insider trading. Understanding how the calculation works, and which of its two limbs applies when, matters more here than almost anywhere else in the enforcement chapter.

## What does section 54(3)(b) actually say?

Section 54(3)(b) of the Act provides that where a legal person wilfully contravenes section 52, the fine is either three times the profit gained or loss avoided as a result of the contravention, or — if that figure cannot be determined — a fine of up to 15% of the body corporate's total annual turnover in the preceding financial year. The text reads:

> "(b) in the case of a legal person, with fine or three times the amount of any profit gained or loss avoided as a result of the contravention. If the amount of profit gained or loss avoided cannot be determined, a fine not exceeding 15% of the total annual turnover of the body corporate in the preceding financial year."

This sits inside section 54(3), which sets the overall penalty for wilful contravention of section 52. Section 54(3)(a) covers natural persons, with a fixed ceiling of three years' imprisonment or a fine up to twenty-five million Rupees, or both. Section 54(3)(b) covers legal persons and abandons the fixed-fine model entirely in favour of two turnover-and-profit-linked calculations.

## Which of the two limbs applies — profit-based or turnover-based?

The Act sets a clear order of operations, not a choice between two equal options. The primary measure is three times the profit gained or loss avoided. The 15%-of-turnover measure is a fallback, available only "if the amount of profit gained or loss avoided cannot be determined."

| Limb | Basis | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | 3x profit gained or loss avoided | Whenever that figure can be determined |
| Fallback | Up to 15% of total annual turnover, preceding financial year | Only if the profit or loss figure cannot be determined |

In practice, this means a regulator or court investigating [market manipulation or insider trading](https://www.coinconnect.site/regulatory-licensing) by a licensed exchange or issuer would first attempt to quantify the actual financial benefit the company gained, or the loss it avoided, from the contravention — for example, the price impact of a manipulative order book pattern, or the trading gain from acting on inside information ahead of an issuance announcement. Only where that figure genuinely cannot be established does the turnover-based fallback come into play.

## How would the profit-based calculation work?

Take a simplified, entirely hypothetical illustration. Suppose a licensed exchange operator is found to have contravened section 52 by trading on inside information ahead of a token listing, and the trading gain attributable to that conduct is established at Rs 4 million. Three times that figure is Rs 12 million — the fine under the primary limb of section 54(3)(b).

Now suppose the same conduct instead avoided a loss — the company held a position and, using inside information about a forthcoming redemption suspension, exited before the loss materialised, avoiding Rs 10 million in losses it would otherwise have taken. Three times that avoided-loss figure is Rs 30 million. The section treats "profit gained" and "loss avoided" as equivalent bases for the same 3x multiplier — a company does not need to have made money to face the fine; avoiding a loss through the same prohibited conduct triggers the identical calculation.

These figures are illustrative only, constructed to show how the multiplier works — they are not drawn from any real matter, and the Act does not itself supply worked examples.

## How would the turnover-based fallback work?

Where the profit or loss figure genuinely cannot be determined — for instance, where manipulative conduct affected market-wide pricing in a way that cannot be isolated to a specific gain or avoided loss for the company itself — the fine shifts to up to 15% of the body corporate's total annual turnover in the preceding financial year.

For a mid-sized [licensed exchange](https://www.coinconnect.site/regulatory-licensing) with, say, Rs 500 million in annual turnover, 15% is Rs 75 million — already exceeding the Rs 50 million ceiling that applies to the most serious fixed-fine offence in the Act, wilfully providing an unlicensed [virtual asset](https://www.coinconnect.site/blog/coinconnect-insights-1/pvara-license-pakistan-complete-guide-22) service under section 54(1). For a larger operator with several billion Rupees in turnover, the fallback figure scales proportionally, with no cap of its own beyond the 15% rate. This is why the corporate limb of section 54(3)(b) is, in Rupee terms, the largest financial exposure anywhere in the Act for any [virtual asset service provider](https://www.coinconnect.site/regulatory-licensing) that generates meaningful revenue.

## Why does the Act treat companies differently from individuals here?

The Act does not state its reasoning within section 54(3)(b) itself, so this is analysis rather than the text of the section. A fixed Rupee ceiling — twenty-five million Rupees, applied to a natural person under section 54(3)(a) — is a meaningful deterrent against an individual. Applied to a large corporate entity with hundreds of millions or billions of Rupees in turnover, the same fixed figure could be an acceptable cost of doing business rather than a deterrent. Tying the corporate penalty to the company's own profit, avoided loss, or turnover scales the fine to the size of the entity being punished, which is a common design pattern in financial-markets legislation internationally, though the Act does not cite that rationale explicitly.

## Is this the only uncapped penalty in the Act?

Yes. Every other criminal fine in section 54 — for unlicensed services, unauthorised offerings, false statements, obstruction, and non-compliance with an order — carries a fixed Rupee ceiling, from one million up to fifty million. The section 59 administrative sanction power is also capped, at twenty-five million Rupees under section 59(4). Section 54(3)(b)'s turnover-based fallback is the only provision in the Act with no absolute Rupee ceiling of its own; its only limit is the percentage rate applied to the company's own financial results.

## What should an operator do about this exposure?

The practical implication is that a licensed [exchange](https://www.coinconnect.site/regulatory-licensing) or [broker-dealer](https://www.coinconnect.site/blog/coinconnect-insights-1/pvara-broker-dealer-license-what-it-covers-27) with an order book, or an [issuer](https://www.coinconnect.site/regulatory-licensing) with material trading activity around its own token, has a direct financial incentive — beyond the general obligation under section 41 to conduct business honestly and fairly — to invest early in market surveillance, information barriers, and [conflict-of-interest management](https://www.coinconnect.site/regulatory-licensing) under section 44. Because the fine scales with the company's own turnover, it grows automatically as the business grows, which argues for building the compliance controls into the operating model at [corporate setup](https://www.coinconnect.site/corporate-setup) stage rather than layering them on after the business — and its turnover — has already scaled.

It is also worth noting that section 54(3)(b) applies specifically to contraventions of section 52. It does not extend the profit-or-turnover calculation to any other offence in the Act — the algorithmic token prohibition under section 53, for example, is not covered by this formula at all, and carries no dedicated criminal penalty of its own.

## Flags for Malik

1. The Act does not define how "profit gained or loss avoided" is to be calculated or evidenced — no methodology, valuation date, or attribution standard is set out in section 54(3)(b) or elsewhere in the source documents. I described the mechanism without inventing a calculation standard.
2. The drafting "with fine or three times the amount" (rather than "with fine of three times the amount") appears to be the same drafting point flagged in our general penalties piece — I have read it as "fine of three times" throughout, consistent with that earlier analysis.
3. Whether the 15% turnover fallback is capped in aggregate by anything outside section 54(3)(b) itself — for example, whether section 59's twenty-five million Rupee administrative cap could apply in parallel or is superseded — is not addressed in the Act as reviewed. Worth flagging to a litigator before citing this as an absolute uncapped exposure in client-facing material.
